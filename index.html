NPS Tier 1: The “No Restriction, Tax‑Free” Pitch
— And What’s Really Under the Hood

A close read of what changed in NPS this year, and what the fine print still says.

Overview

Retirement planning has moved from an afterthought to a headline topic, helped along by the rise of FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) culture and a growing appetite for building a corpus that actually lasts. Every version of that conversation eventually lands on the same question: which vehicle carries the money best? Lately, NPS Tier 1 has been getting an outsized share of that attention.

The pitch usually goes something like this: park your retirement money in NPS Tier 1, ride the equity upside, walk away with up to 80% as a lump sum, and enjoy greater flexibility than before. On paper, it can look like the closest thing to a free lunch in Indian personal finance. And the NPS framework has genuinely undergone significant reform. But the fine print matters — particularly when regulatory flexibility and tax treatment don’t move at the same speed.

Two reforms behind the buzz

What actually changed — and what didn’t

Two changes explain why NPS is suddenly looking considerably more attractive. Tap through to see what moved, and where the limits still stand.

But not disappeared. Under the standard Active Choice framework, Tier 1 equity exposure is still capped at 75% up to age 50, tapering down after that. What’s new is the Multiple Scheme Framework (MSF), launched around NPS Diwas on October 1, 2025, which lets non‑government subscribers opt into a high‑risk scheme variant with up to 100% equity exposure.

That’s a genuine first for NPS, but it’s opt‑in, brand new, and carries no meaningful track record yet. “No restriction” only applies to subscribers who deliberately choose this variant — the default framework remains capped.

On the regulatory side, yes. Previously, non‑government subscribers had to put a mandatory 40% of their corpus into an annuity, taking home only 60% as a lump sum. Under the revised 2026 framework, that mandatory annuity share drops to just 20% for corpuses above ₹12 lakh — and below ₹12 lakh can often be withdrawn in full.

PFRDA now permits up to 80% of the corpus as a lump sum — a meaningful jump in flexibility. And this is where the story gets interesting: PFRDA may have expanded what you can withdraw, but that doesn’t automatically mean the Income Tax Act has expanded what is tax‑free.

Where the tax-free story runs into the fine print

The income-tax exemption under Section 10(12A) is capped at 60% of the corpus, regardless of what PFRDA allows a subscriber to withdraw. That exemption sits in tax law, not pension regulation — and tax law hasn’t yet been amended to match PFRDA’s more generous 80% withdrawal limit.

  • ✓ The first 60% withdrawn as a lump sum is unambiguously tax-free under Section 10(12A).
  • ? The additional 20% that PFRDA now allows as a lump sum — in place of the old mandatory annuity — does not automatically carry the same exemption. Several recent analyses flag this exact mismatch: the regulator permits the withdrawal, but the tax code hasn’t explicitly caught up to it.
  • i Staggering the withdrawal through a Systematic Lump Sum Withdrawal (SLW) doesn’t change this picture. SLW spreads out when the already tax-exempt 60% is received — it doesn’t convert otherwise-taxable money into tax-free money.

The reality check

“80% tax-free” vs. what actually happens

Put together, the realistic split looks less like a single tax-free windfall and more like three separate outcomes. Hover or tap each segment below.

60% — Clearly tax-free

Withdrawn as a lump sum and exempt under Section 10(12A), without ambiguity.

20% — Mandatory annuity

Taxable as pension income for life, at whatever rate the insurer offers — historically in the 6–7% range.

20% — The “extra” lump sum

Now permitted by PFRDA in place of the old annuity requirement, but sitting in a tax grey zone that could well be taxed.

Putting it into a plan

None of this takes away from NPS Tier 1 being a genuinely useful piece of a retirement plan — it simply works best when it’s sized and sequenced around the bigger picture, rather than chosen purely for the tax-free headline.

Understand what kind of corpus retirement will actually require, and let that number — not the headline tax treatment — guide how much goes into NPS Tier 1.

Since the NPS corpus itself stays locked in till 60, it helps to keep parallel investments with full liquidity for goals that can’t wait — medical needs, children’s education, or a wedding — so retirement savings never have to be disturbed early.

The bigger picture

NPS may have become more flexible, but flexibility doesn’t automatically make it the right choice for everyone. The real question is not “How much can I put into NPS?” but “How much do I actually need for the retirement I want?” Your retirement corpus has to balance growth, tax efficiency, liquidity and future income — not just chase a tax benefit. NPS can play an important role, but it is one piece of a much larger financial plan.

Because the best retirement plan isn’t the one with the best tax benefit — it’s the one that supports the life you want to live.
